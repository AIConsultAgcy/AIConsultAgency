FANDS FIELD GUIDE 001 · 7 MIN READ

Your first AI workflow
should be boring.

Start with the handoff your team knows too well. Then make the improvement visible.

The best place to begin is often the work nobody puts in a presentation: copying an approved brief into a project record, checking whether an intake form is complete, or assembling the same kickoff pack for the tenth time.

These tasks have something a big “AI transformation” idea often lacks: a clear beginning, a recognizable output, and a person who can tell whether the result is useful.

1. Find a recurring handoff.

List five moments when work moves between people or tools. For each, record what triggers it, what the next person needs, who owns it, and how often it happens. Start with the queue that causes repeated chasing.

For a consulting firm, that might be the gap between a signed scope and an approved project kickoff. The input is a known document. The output is a reviewed project record, a task list, and a kickoff pack.

2. Score the opportunity.

Give each criterion a score from 0 to 2. This is a prioritization aid, not a validated prediction of success.

Criterion0 points2 points
FrequencyRare or unpredictableRepeated every week
Input readinessMissing or inaccessibleAvailable and consistent
ReviewabilityHard to verifyA person can check quickly
RiskConsequential, irreversibleBounded and reversible
OwnershipNobody owns the processA named process owner

Use 1 point for the middle ground. Investigate the highest score first. A zero on ownership or risk is a reason to pause regardless of the total.

3. Measure the work before you change it.

For a proposed two-week baseline, log every eligible handoff. Track the number of cases, hands-on minutes, elapsed time, missing information, and rework. Use a longer period if volume is too low or seasonality would distort the comparison.

Keep handling time and cycle time separate. Fifteen minutes of work can sit in a queue for three days. A system may improve one measure without improving the other.

4. Put AI in a bounded role.

Ask AI to extract, summarize, classify, or draft from approved sources. Put deterministic rules around required fields, access, and routing. Keep a person responsible for consequential approval.

The system needs an exception path. If a brief is incomplete, contradictory, or low confidence, it should stop and route the case to its owner. It should never quietly invent a missing budget or promise a delivery date.

5. Test the inconvenient cases.

Build a test set that includes incomplete documents, duplicates, unexpected formats, permission failures, and conflicting instructions inside source text. Verify that the workflow does not treat document content as permission to take an action.

A successful happy path is necessary. Reliable failure handling is what makes the workflow usable.

6. Compare on equal terms.

Compare similar cases before and after deployment. Include time spent reviewing outputs, fixing mistakes, and maintaining the system. Report the sample size and the observation period alongside the result.

Illustrative calculation, not a client result

80 handoffs per month × 30 minutes each = 40 hours of manual work. An assumed 30% reduction suggests 12 hours of gross capacity. Subtract additional review and maintenance before describing the net benefit. Reclaimed time becomes cash savings only when a real cost changes.

7. Decide whether it earned the next step.

Agree the decision rule before launch: what quality must be maintained, what improvement matters, and which failures mean rollback. At the review, choose to expand, revise, maintain, or stop.

Your first workflow does not need to transform the company. It needs to prove you can make one important piece of work measurably better.
